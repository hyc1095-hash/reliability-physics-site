為什麼叫「熱」載子？

這一頁不用任何公式，只有一根滑桿。目標是讓你對「電場多強才會出事」建立量感——之後每一頁的數學，都是把這個量感寫成算式而已。

一分鐘版

電子在矽裡前進時會不斷被晶格撞散，平均每走 λ ≈ 9 奈米就被撞一次，把好不容易累積的動能還給晶格，等於重新歸零。 在兩次碰撞之間，電場給它的能量大約是 qλE。 當這個能量接近 1 eV 以上，電子就「熱」得足以做壞事 —— 這就是熱載子（「熱」講的是能量高，不是溫度高）。

電子在通道裡並不是一路加速

直覺上，電場推著電子跑，電子應該越跑越快。但矽晶格裡到處都是振動的原子（聲子）， 電子平均每走一小段距離就會被撞一次，把好不容易累積的能量交出去，然後從頭開始。

這個「平均走多遠被撞一次」的距離叫 平均自由程 λ，在室溫的矽裡大約 9 奈米——比一根頭髮的萬分之一還細。 所以真正決定電子能不能變「熱」的，不是它走了多遠，而是它一口氣不被打斷能撐多久：

關鍵直覺

一次自由飛行能拿到多少能量 = q × λ × E
電場 E 越強，或平均自由程 λ 越長，單次飛行拿到的能量就越多。這個能量要跟三道「門檻」比大小。

三道門檻

拖動下面的電場滑桿，看能量長條越過哪幾道線。越過門檻不代表一定會發生，而是機率變得不可忽略 —— 有些幸運的電子連續好幾個自由程都沒被撞到，就能累積到門檻以上。滑桿數值本身不用背，重點是看三條機率曲線怎麼隨電場動。

Stage 1 / 7 · Intuition

Why "hot" carriers?

No formulas on this page — just one slider. The goal is to build a feel for how strong a field has to be before things go wrong; every equation on the pages that follow is just this same intuition written down.

The 1-minute version

As an electron moves through silicon, the lattice keeps knocking it around — on average once every λ ≈ 9 nm, handing back whatever kinetic energy it had picked up and starting over. Between two collisions, the field hands it roughly qλE of energy. Once that energy gets close to 1 eV, the electron is "hot" enough to cause trouble — that's a hot carrier ("hot" means high-energy, not high-temperature).

Electrons don't just keep accelerating down the channel

Intuitively, the field pushes the electron along, so it should just keep speeding up. But the silicon lattice is full of vibrating atoms (phonons), and on average the electron gets knocked around after every short stretch, handing back whatever energy it had built up and starting from zero again.

This "average distance between hits" is called the mean free path λ, and in room-temperature silicon it's roughly 9 nm — about a ten-thousandth the width of a human hair. So what actually decides whether an electron goes "hot" isn't how far it has traveled — it's how long it manages to fly uninterrupted:

The key intuition

Energy from one free flight = q × λ × E
A stronger field E, or a longer mean free path λ, means more energy per flight. That energy then gets compared against three "thresholds."

Three thresholds

Drag the field slider below and watch which lines the energy bar crosses. Crossing a threshold doesn't guarantee the event — it means the probability stops being negligible — some lucky electrons go several mean free paths in a row without a hit, and that's how they end up above threshold. Don't worry about memorizing the numbers; just watch how the three probability curves move with the field.

為什麼工程師在乎

界面態 Nit 會讓臨界電壓 Vt 漂移、電流下降、轉導 gm 變差。 單一個缺陷不算什麼，但元件一天工作幾兆個週期，缺陷持續累積，而且不會自己好。 可靠度工程的問題就是：照這個速率累積下去，十年後元件還在規格內嗎？——這正是後面幾頁要教你算的東西。

檢查你懂了嗎

如果我把通道電流加倍，HCI 損傷會加倍嗎？
不會等比例。能量門檻由電場決定，跟有多少電子在跑無關。電流加倍只是讓「嘗試次數」加倍，但每次嘗試的成功機率完全沒變。相對地，電場只要提高一點，指數項 e−φ/qλE 就會大幅上升。所以降 VD 的效果遠大於降 ID。
為什麼「平均能量」遠低於 1.3 eV，卻還是會發生撞擊游離？
因為散射是隨機的。平均每 λ 被撞一次，但總有一些電子連續走了 5、6 個 λ 都沒被撞到，累積的能量就是平均值的 5、6 倍。這些「幸運電子」就是損傷的來源 —— 這也是 lucky electron model 名稱的由來。
猜猜看：溫度升高，HCI 會變嚴重還是變輕微？
變輕微（對傳統的通道熱載子而言）。溫度高 → 晶格振動劇烈 → 電子更容易被撞散 → λ 變短 → 同樣的電場下 qλE 變小 → 幸運電子更少。你可以現在就拖溫度滑桿驗證：三個機率都會下降。

這件事的正式說法是「HCI 的活化能是負的」，跟 NBTI、TDDB 相反 —— 階段 3 和 4 會算給你看。
那為什麼不乾脆讓元件在高溫下工作就好？
因為其他退化機制（NBTI、TDDB、電遷移）都是正活化能，高溫會惡化。實務上要在多個機制之間取平衡，而且 HCI 的 worst case 通常就落在規格的低溫端 —— 這是很多人做 qual 時會忽略的角落。

Why engineers care

Interface traps Nit shift the threshold voltage Vt, cut the drive current, and degrade transconductance gm. One defect is nothing, but a device runs trillions of cycles a day, so defects keep piling up — and they never heal on their own. The reliability-engineering question is: if it keeps accumulating at this rate, will the device still be within spec in ten years? — which is exactly what the next few pages teach you to calculate.

Three takeaways to keep

IdeaWhy it matters
HCI is a field problem
not a current problem		 Doubling the current doesn't touch the energy threshold at all; but a mere 20% increase in field can jump the event probability by an order of magnitude. Lowering VD is far more effective than lowering ID.
It runs on "luck" The average energy sits well below threshold — the damage comes from the rare electrons in the tail of the distribution. That's why every HCI formula carries an exponential term like e−φ/qλE.
The damage never heals A broken Si–H bond stays broken. Unlike NBTI — which partially recovers once stress is removed, HCI doesn't. That difference changes how you have to do the entire lifetime calculation.

Check your understanding

If I double the channel current, does HCI damage double too?
No, not proportionally. The energy threshold is set by the field, not by how many electrons are flowing. Doubling the current just doubles the number of "attempts," but the success probability per attempt doesn't change at all. A field increase, on the other hand, drives the exponential term e−φ/qλE sharply upward. That's why lowering VD beats lowering ID by a wide margin.
Why does impact ionization still happen when the "average energy" is well below 1.3 eV?
Because scattering is random. On average an electron gets hit once every λ, but some electrons travel 5 or 6 λ in a row without a hit, ending up with 5-6× the average energy. These "lucky electrons" are the source of the damage — which is exactly where the name "lucky electron model" comes from.
Guess: does raising the temperature make HCI worse or better?
Better (for classic channel hot carriers). Higher temperature → more violent lattice vibration → electrons get scattered more easily → λ shrinks → for the same field, qλE drops → fewer lucky electrons. You can verify this right now by dragging the temperature slider: all three probabilities fall.

The formal way to say this is "HCI has a negative activation energy," the opposite of NBTI and TDDB — Stages 3 and 4 will walk you through computing it.
So why not just run the device hot on purpose?
Because the other degradation mechanisms (NBTI, TDDB, electromigration) all have positive activation energy and get worse with heat. In practice you have to balance multiple mechanisms against each other — and HCI's worst case usually sits at the low-temperature end of the spec, a corner a lot of people overlook when planning qualification.
